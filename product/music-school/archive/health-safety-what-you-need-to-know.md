**HEALTH AND SAFETY ****- What you need to know **

**INTRODUCTION TO HEALTH AND SAFETY **

Identifying your health and safety duties can be a difficult process. Add to this the challenge of determining exactly how you are going to discharge these duties and it is easy to see why so many businesses struggle to comply with their health and safety obligations despite the obvious benefits for their workers and business.

Before considering what your health and safety obligations are, two concepts must first be understood.
	Who are your workers (ie to whom do you owe health and safety obligations)?
	Where is your workplace (ie where do your health and safety obligations apply)?

**KEY CONCEPT: WORKERS **

As a business, the health and safety obligations you owe are generally expressed to apply to ‘workers’.  This concept is broader than the traditional concept of an employee. It covers anyone carrying out work in any capacity for you, including employees, labour hire employees, contractors, interns and volunteers.

In addition, you owe health and safety obligations to others who may enter your workplace such as customers, clients and suppliers.

**KEY CONCEPT: THE WORKPLACE **

The concept of ‘the workplace’ is also much broader than you might expect. For health and safety purposes, a workplace is anywhere that work is carried out, or anywhere that a worker goes while at work.

When we refer to your workplace, we are not just talking about your primary place of business (for example, your office). Other places that may be considered part of your workplace include:

	Any motor vehicle that your tutors travel in or use in the course of their duties;
	Any third party client sites that your workers use or visit in the course of their duties, e.g a persons home, or a school
	Even the workers own home if they are a home-worker or are otherwise tutoring from home.

**YOUR HEALTH AND SAFETY OBLIGATIONS **

Your overarching obligation is to provide a safe workplace for workers. However, meeting this obligation is not as simple as it may seem.

**GUIDE TO EMPLOYMENT SERVICES **

A safe workplace is best achieved by taking a hazard management approach that focuses on identifying, assessing and controlling hazards, and then continually reviewing this. Further guidance on this approach is set out in the hazard management section of this guide.
In addition to undertaking hazard management, you must:

	Provide and maintain a safe working environment
	Provide and maintain safe systems of work;
Provide the information, training, instruction and supervision necessary to protect workers from risks to their health and safety.

In the event you do not meet any of the above obligations, you and your school may be subject to enforcement measures such as improvement notices, on-the-spot fines, court ordered financial penalties and even criminal prosecution and imprisonment.

**‘****AS FAR AS REASONABLY PRACTICABLE****’ **

Each of the above obligations apply ‘as far as reasonably practicable’.

Determining what is reasonably practicable for your business requires consideration of the hazards your workers face, the likelihood of a hazard or risk occurring, and the potential harm that a hazard or risk may result in. It requires consideration of what options are available and suitable to eliminate or minimise that type of hazard or risk. The cost of control measures, the effectiveness of these measures and the size of your business will also be taken into account.

Despite this, the obligation on you is very high. Health and safety needs to be your top priority, and it is expected that you will do anything necessary to protect your workers from health and safety hazards and risks.

**DUTIES OF MANAGERS AND SUPERVISORS **

You should encourage your team to play an active role in achieving a safe workplace.

Just as you have health and safety obligations as an owner of a business, your managers and supervisors also have obligations under the law. Their obligations focus on maintaining a safe working environment and systems of work. They should achieve this by identifying hazards in the workplace and ensuring these are addressed, as well as implementing any required training and supervision of workers.

If you have Managers and supervisors, they should also play a key role in consulting with your team about health and safety matters. They should set a good example on health and safety matters for the rest of the workplace to follow, and ensure that everyone in the workplace is complying with health and safety rules and procedures.

In the event a manager or supervisor does not comply with these obligations, they may be personally liable for enforcement measures such as court ordered financial penalties and even criminal prosecution and imprisonment.

**DUTIES OF WORKERS **

Your workers have health and safety responsibilities too, which they owe to themselves and others in the workplace.

Your workers must take reasonable care for their own health and safety, and reasonable care that their acts or omissions do not adversely affect the health and safety of others.

In addition, workers must comply with any reasonable instruction, policy or procedure that you have communicated to them regarding health and safety.

For a worker to follow your instructions, policies and procedures, these must be known to them. You must train your workers in your health and safety procedures and practices, and ensure that these are consistently followed. This training should be provided when they start working for you and repeated as necessary. It is important that you keep a record of any training that has been provided to workers.

**CONSULTATION**
Workers are often in the best position to identify health and safety hazards relating to their specific job, tasks, processes or workplace. Likewise, identifying solutions in conjunction with workers is the most effective way of controlling hazards in the workplace.

For these reasons, you are required to engage in consultation with your workers on health and safety matters.

This requires you to open a dialogue with your workers focused on the sharing of health and safety information, and providing an opportunity for workers to express their views and contribute to health and safety decisions you make.

There are two formal means of consultation that are regulated by law: the appointment of health and safety representatives, and forming of health and safety committees. However, in many workplaces this level of consultation will not be required (unless requested by your workers). In these circumstances, engaging in less formal means of consultation will be sufficient to fulfil your obligations.

**HEALTH AND SAFETY REPRESENTATIVES **

Workers are entitled to request that a health and safety representative be elected to represent their work group or workplace on health and safety matters.

Once a request is made, you must take certain steps to facilitate the election or appointment of a worker to this role. Generally, a workplace election will be held to determine who is appointed to this role, unless there is only one candidate.

Once appointed, you are required to consult with health and safety representatives on health and safety matters relating to their work group or workplace.

Health and safety representatives have various powers, including to represent their work group on health and safety issues, monitor health and safety in the workplace, and investigate health and safety hazards or complaints.

**DOCUMENTING CONSULTATION **

In order to demonstrate that you have discharged your obligation to consult with workers, you need to keep records demonstrating what consultation has been undertaken.

These records can be in any format, provided they show when consultation has taken place, what matters have been discussed and what decisions have been communicated back to the workers.

In addition, you should keep note of discussions with health and safety representatives, and minutes of any meetings of a health and safety committee. We store ours in google docs, very simple.

**HAZARD MANAGEMENT**

A hazard is any situation or object that has the potential to harm a person As outlined above, one of your key obligations is to identify, assess and control hazards in the workplace, in order to eliminate or minimise potential risks to the health and safety of your workers. This process is known as hazard management.
You need to undertake hazard management when starting a new business, changing work practices, purchasing new equipment or substances, in response to incidents and concerns raised by workers, and at any time when new information about an existing hazard in your workplace becomes available.

This process should be repeated on an ongoing basis as required to continually improve levels of health and safety within your business.

Workers should be consulted throughout each stage of this process.

**IDENTIFYING HAZARDS **

The first step in the hazard management process involves identifying hazards.
Hazards can be identified by observing the workplace and work tasks as well as equipment, materials and substances used in your workplace.

Take note of anything that may cause harm to a person. Consider potential harm caused when a worker is following the correct or accepted work methods, and predict hazards that might arise if a worker deviates from these methods.

If there has been an incident or injury in the past, this can also help you to identify hazards.

**ASSESSING RISKS **

Once you have identified hazards, the next step is to assess the risks associated with these.
This requires you to evaluate the hazard to determine:

	How likely it is that the hazard will result in harm (ranking this from rare to almost certain); and
	How serious is the harm that the hazard may result in (ranking this from marginal to severe).

**STEP 3: CONTROLLING RISKS **

Once hazards have been identified and assessed, the next step is to consider how you can control these. Prioritise this step for hazards that have been assessed as high or medium risk.

Your focus should be on eliminating these hazards wherever possible, as this will ultimately lead to the highest levels of health and safety in the workplace.

If you cannot eliminate a hazard, consider how you can minimise the associated risks to workers. This may be achieved through various measures, including:

	Substituting the hazard with something safer (for example, a non-toxic cleaning product);
	Isolating the hazard from workers (for example, putting a wet floor sign up)

Remember that you just need to do what is reasonably practicable in light of the nature of the hazard and associated risks. As a result, for hazards with a high likelihood of occurrence, or that pose a serious risk to the worker, your obligations are quite high, even if this comes at a substantial cost to your business or is quite difficult to implement.

**STEP 4: REVIEWING CONTROLS **

Once you have implemented measures to control a hazard, you need to review these on a regular basis.

This review should focus on the effectiveness of the control, identifying whether any more effective controls are available, considering the impact of any changes in the workplace on the control, identifying new hazards that may have been introduced and considering the feedback of workers.

**DOCUMENTING HAZARD MANAGEMENT **

You need to document the hazard management process. Not only will this help to demonstrate that you are complying with your obligations, it will also assist you to monitor and continually improve levels of health and safety in your workplace.

**RESPONDING TO INCIDENTS **

An incident is any situation to which a worker is exposed that results in personal injury or property damage. It also includes a situation where a ‘near miss’ occurs – that is, where a worker is exposed to a situation of this nature, but personal injury or property damage does not eventuate.
Following the hazard management approach outlined above will assist in preventing incidents. Where an incident does occur, it can be a sign that your hazard management approach has not been effective and you may need to revisit this process.

The below outlines your key obligations in responding to incidents. While the majority of these relate to incidents where an injury to a worker has resulted, keep in mind that even if an incident does not result in an injury, you may still have obligations regarding how you respond to this.

**IMMEDIATE RESPONSE **

Your immediate response to any incident in the workplace should be to take immediate steps to ensure the safety of the workplace and workers. The action required here will depend on the circumstances. An example might be to remove faulty tools from the workplace, blocking access to broken machinery until it can be fixed, or turning off the electricity supply.

Where an incident has resulted in an illness or injury to a worker, you must also provide the worker with the appropriate first aid as part of your immediate response.

In the case of a minor illness or injury, this may be as simple as providing the worker with first aid supplies from your first aid kit. In more serious cases, the worker may need to be administered first aid by a trained first aid officer, transported to a doctor or medical centre for medical treatment, or even be provided with emergency medical treatment via an ambulance or hospital emergency department.

**REPORTING OBLIGATIONS **

Where an incident occurs, you will need to consider whether you have any external reporting obligations in relation to this incident.

You must report any injury that may result in a workers compensation claim to your workers compensation insurer, as detailed later in this guide.

In addition, you are required to report incidents that involve a serious illness, injury or death to the regulator. You are also required to report any dangerous incident or event (such as a fire, explosion or electric shock) to the regulator.

**PROVIDING FIRST AID **

One of the most common responses to an incident will be the provision of first aid.

First aid is the immediate treatment or care given to a person suffering from an injury or illness until more advanced care is provided or the person recovers.

You have an obligation to provide first aid equipment in your workplace (for example, a first aid kit), along with facilities for the administration of first aid and to make persons available at the workplace who are trained in administering first aid.

The level of first aid resources required in your workplace will depend on a number of factors, including the type of work your workers perform, the potential risks to the health and safety of your workers, and the accessibility of external medical facilities from your workplace.

You should conduct a hazard assessment taking factors such as these into account to assist you in identifying what first aid equipment, facilities and personnel you require.

**INVESTIGATION OBLIGATIONS **

Where an incident occurs in the workplace, you should conduct an investigation into this as soon as reasonably practicable. This will involve identifying the causes of the incident and any control measures that can be implemented to prevent re-occurrence.

Workers should be actively consulted as part of this investigation, and any changes that are implemented to improve health and safety as a result of an incident should be communicated back to workers.

**DOCUMENTING INCIDENT RESPONSE **

You need to keep records to demonstrate how you have responded to workplace incidents.
Details of an incident and your investigation into this should be recorded via an incident and hazard report form.

Where an incident involves an injury, the injury should be recorded on a Register of Injuries.
