**Project Management**

"The Goal" says that only one part of any process really matters, **the bottleneck**. The bottleneck is whichever part of the process can handle the least amount of material. Nothing else matters, except if it holds up the bottleneck. The logic is fairly easy to see, but the implications are not.
Suppose you have a process that goes like this: Raw materials are cleaned (10 minutes per 10 units)-> cleaned items are machined (10 minutes per 1 unit)-> machined items are heat treated (10 minutes per 10 units)-> heat treated items are shipped (1 minute per 10 units).
Now suppose that you started with an empty plant and ran the process for an hour. Your output would look like this.
Minute 10: 10 cleaned items
Minute 20: 19 cleaned items, 1 machined item
Minute 30: 28 cleaned items, 1 machined item, 1 heat treated item
Minute 40: 37 cleaned items, 1 machined item, 1 heat treated item, 1 shipped item
Minute 50: 46 cleaned items, 1 machined item, 1 heat treated item, 2 shipped items
Minute 60: 55 cleaned items, 1 machined item, 1 heat treated item, 3 shipped items
Run the plant for 8 hours and you get 45 units shipped per day.
Even though you can ship items at 100 times the speed that you can machine them, *that doesn't matter.* You are still shipping at the rate of the bottleneck, 1 unit per 10 minutes.
If you ordered raw materials that were already cleaned, completely skipping those 10 minutes for every product, then you would ship exactly 46 units per day.
If you instead invent an 1 second per 1 million units heat-treat device, you, again, ship 46 units per day.
But if you buy another machine and double the capacity of your machining (the bottleneck), you ship *90 units per day.* The bottleneck is the only thing that matters. The rate at which you get product out of the door is the same as the rate at which your bottleneck operates. Saving time on the bottleneck is multiplicative; anywhere else is additive.

So why did Alex have so many customer back-orders? Because he wasn't focusing on his bottleneck, and that strangled his production.
But then why did Alex have an inventory level that was out of control? Because there were products that could be machined very quickly, or not at all, and Alex hated to see people idle.
A very common rule in business is something to the effect of "Work on the high-priority stuff. If you don't have any high-priority stuff, work on the low priority stuff." The book argues that this is often a bad rule to follow.
By spending your time making products that you already have in vast quantities, you are doing nothing but increasing inventory levels, and necessitating the purchase of more raw materials. In other words, by "keeping everyone busy" you are actually losing money.
Instead of working on "low-priority" tasks, the author argues that the bottleneck should set the pace. Anything else is counter-productive. And naturally, never spend the bottleneck's time on something that is low-priority.

That is the two main points of the book: "Focus exclusively on the bottleneck", and "Let the bottleneck set the pace."
